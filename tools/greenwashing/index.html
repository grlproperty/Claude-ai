Greenwashing Decoder

A reference for the language brands use when they want to suggest virtue without committing to it. Each term shows what brands typically claim and what the term actually means.

Natural

general

What brands imply

Often implies “good for you” or “safe”, suggesting wholesome plant origin.

What it actually means

In most jurisdictions there is no legal definition of “natural” in cosmetics or food labelling. Arsenic, lead, and poison ivy are also “natural”.

Source — US FTC: “no clear definition”; EU food law: no harmonised meaning.

Eco-friendly

general

What brands imply

The product is gentle on the environment.

What it actually means

Considered too vague to substantiate by the US FTC. The Green Guides require any environmental claim be qualified with what specifically is eco-friendly and how.

Source — FTC Green Guides §260.4.

Biodegradable

waste

What brands imply

The product breaks down naturally and harmlessly.

What it actually means

Must be qualified — biodegradable under what conditions? Within what timeframe? Most “biodegradable” plastics require industrial composting (60°C+) which most consumers cannot access.

Source — FTC Green Guides §260.8; EU EN 13432 standard.

Compostable

waste

What brands imply

You can compost the packaging at home.

What it actually means

Usually means industrially compostable, not home-compostable. Distinguish between BPI / TUV “OK Compost HOME” certification and “OK Compost INDUSTRIAL”.

Source — EU EN 13432 (industrial); EN 17427 (home).

Recyclable

waste

What brands imply

This product can be recycled.

What it actually means

Only meaningful if recycling facilities for the specific material are available to most consumers in your region. Theoretically recyclable ≠ practically recycled. Globally, less than 9% of all plastic ever made has been recycled.

Source — OECD Global Plastics Outlook 2022.

Cruelty-free

animals

What brands imply

No animals were harmed.

What it actually means

Not legally defined globally. May refer to the finished product but not individual ingredients, or only to the manufacturer’s own labs not third-party testing. Look for Leaping Bunny or PETA Beauty Without Bunnies certification.

Source — EU Cosmetics Reg banned animal-tested cosmetics from sale since 2013; cruelty-free labelling unregulated.

Vegan

animals

What brands imply

Contains no animal-derived ingredients.

What it actually means

In cosmetics, not legally defined — but a “vegan” label generally means no animal-derived ingredients. It does NOT mean cruelty-free.

Source — Vegan Society / Vegan Action certification.

Sustainable

general

What brands imply

Doesn’t harm the environment over time.

What it actually means

No regulatory definition. The UN Brundtland definition is “meeting the needs of the present without compromising future generations” — meaningless as a product label.

Source — UN Brundtland Commission (1987).

Carbon-neutral

climate

What brands imply

The company has zero net carbon emissions.

What it actually means

Almost always achieved through carbon offsets, not actual emission reductions. Many offset schemes have been investigated for over-claiming. The EU proposes banning the term unless backed by science-based reductions.

Source — EU Green Claims Directive (2023 proposal); Guardian/Die Zeit/SourceMaterial Verra investigation (2023).

Net-zero

climate

What brands imply

Net carbon emissions of zero.

What it actually means

Usually depends on offsets and assumed future negative-emissions technology. The SBTi Net-Zero Standard sets criteria (90%+ absolute reduction by 2050) but few corporate “net-zero” claims meet it.

Source — Science Based Targets initiative (SBTi).

Plant-based

food

What brands imply

Made primarily from plants.

What it actually means

No regulatory threshold. “Plant-based” can mean 100% plant or “contains some plants” depending on the product.

Source — FDA: no enforced definition.

Clean / Clean beauty

beauty

What brands imply

Free of harmful or “bad” ingredients.

What it actually means

No regulatory definition. Each retailer (Sephora, Credo, Ulta) sets its own “clean” criteria — they don’t agree with each other.

Source — No regulatory standard.

Free-range

animals

What brands imply

Animals roamed freely outdoors.

What it actually means

In the EU, “free-range” eggs require 4m² per hen of outdoor access — but only theoretical access; many never go outside. For meat, the term is less regulated. UK definitions are similar.

Source — EU Reg 589/2008 (eggs); UK DEFRA.

Organic

food

What brands imply

Grown without synthetic chemicals.

What it actually means

Legally defined in EU and US — requires certification (USDA Organic, EU Organic logo). The term itself, alone, on a product is regulated. But “organic” cosmetic claims are less tightly controlled.

Source — EU Reg 2018/848; USDA NOP.

Fair trade

social

What brands imply

Workers received fair wages and conditions.

What it actually means

Several certifications exist (Fairtrade International, Fair Trade USA, World Fair Trade Organization) with different standards. “Fair trade” without certification is meaningless.

Source — Fairtrade International standards.

Locally sourced

general

What brands imply

Came from nearby.

What it actually means

No regulatory definition of “local”. Some EU/UK retailers define it as within 30 miles or within county — but the term on packaging is unregulated.

Source — UK ASA case law.

Non-toxic

beauty

What brands imply

Will not cause harm.

What it actually means

Dose-dependent and undefined. The FTC Green Guides require substantiation for non-toxic claims; the EU has prohibited the unqualified use of “non-toxic” on detergents and cleaning products.

Source — FTC Green Guides §260.10; EU Detergents Regulation.

Greener / More sustainable

general

What brands imply

Less harmful than the alternative.

What it actually means

Comparative claims require disclosure of the comparison basis. “Greener than what?” “More sustainable than which version?” — must be substantiated.

Source — FTC Green Guides §260.4; UK CMA Green Claims Code 2021.

Made with renewable energy

climate

What brands imply

The factory runs on solar/wind etc.

What it actually means

May refer to purchased Renewable Energy Certificates (RECs) that don’t actually power the factory. Look for guarantees of additionality and physical delivery.

Source — EU Green Claims Directive (2023 proposal).

Conscious / Mindful

general

What brands imply

Thoughtfully made.

What it actually means

Marketing language only. No regulatory meaning. The UK ASA and EU Green Claims Directive both flag these as unsubstantiated vagueness.

Source — EU Green Claims Directive (2023 proposal).

Ethically sourced

social

What brands imply

Materials and labour meet fair standards.

What it actually means

No legal definition and no required certification. Without a named standard (Fairtrade, SA8000, WFTO) it is an unverifiable marketing phrase.

Source — UK CMA Green Claims Code 2021.

Responsibly sourced

social

What brands imply

Sourcing considers people and planet.

What it actually means

Undefined and self-declared. ‘Responsible’ sets no measurable bar — ask: responsible to whom, verified by whom?

Source — EU Green Claims Directive (2023 proposal).

Reef-safe

climate

What brands imply

This sunscreen won’t harm coral reefs.

What it actually means

There is no standardised or regulated definition of ‘reef-safe’. Check the actual filters — avoid oxybenzone and octinoxate rather than trusting the label.

Source — US NOAA / Hawaii Act 104.

Ocean-bound plastic

waste

What brands imply

Made from plastic rescued from the sea.

What it actually means

Usually means plastic collected within ~50 km of a coastline — not from the ocean itself. Definitions vary by certifier and are often generous.

Source — Zero Plastic Oceans / OBP certification.

Made with recycled materials

waste

What brands imply

The product is recycled.

What it actually means

Can mean as little as 1% recycled content. Look for a stated percentage and whether it is post-consumer (used by people) or pre-consumer (factory offcuts).

Source — ISO 14021 / FTC Green Guides.

Chemical-free

beauty

What brands imply

Contains no chemicals.

What it actually means

Scientifically impossible — water is a chemical. The term is meaningless and flagged as misleading by regulators.

Source — UK ASA rulings; EU Green Claims Directive.

Dermatologist-tested

beauty

What brands imply

Proven safe by skin doctors.

What it actually means

Only means a dermatologist was involved in testing — not that it passed, nor how many people, nor the result. ‘Tested’ is not ‘approved’.

Source — US FDA cosmetics guidance.

Hypoallergenic

beauty

What brands imply

Won’t cause allergic reactions.

What it actually means

No federal standard or required testing in the US. Manufacturers define it themselves. Any product can be labelled hypoallergenic.

Source — US FDA: ‘no meaning’.

Grass-fed

food

What brands imply

The animal ate only grass, on pasture.

What it actually means

In many markets animals may be ‘grass-fed’ yet grain-finished, or fed grass indoors. ‘100% grass-fed’ and pasture certifications are stricter.

Source — USDA (withdrew its grass-fed standard in 2016).

Cage-free

animals

What brands imply

Hens roam freely, not in cages.

What it actually means

Means hens are uncaged inside a barn — not that they go outdoors or have much space. Distinct from free-range or pasture-raised.

Source — EU Reg 589/2008; RSPCA Assured standards.

Humanely raised

animals

What brands imply

Animals had good welfare.

What it actually means

Not legally defined in most markets. Meaningful only with a third-party welfare certification (e.g. RSPCA Assured, Certified Humane, GAP).

Source — No regulatory standard.

No nasties

beauty

What brands imply

Free from harmful ingredients.

What it actually means

Marketing shorthand with no definition. Each brand decides what a ‘nasty’ is — often to imply competitors are unsafe.

Source — UK ASA / EU Green Claims Directive.

Tree-free

waste

What brands imply

No trees were cut for this paper.

What it actually means

Can be true (bamboo, sugarcane bagasse) but doesn’t address the water, land, or transport footprint of the alternative crop. Verify the whole picture.

Source — FTC Green Guides §260.4.

Climate positive / Climate neutral

climate

What brands imply

The product removes more carbon than it emits.

What it actually means

Almost always relies on offsets rather than actual removals, and ‘positive’ claims are even harder to substantiate than ‘neutral’ ones. The EU proposes restricting both.

Source — EU Green Claims Directive (2023 proposal).

Palm-oil free

climate

What brands imply

Contains no palm oil, so it’s better for forests.

What it actually means

Sometimes true, but replacements (coconut, soy, shea) can need MORE land. Certified sustainable palm (RSPO) can be a lower-impact choice than a blanket boycott.

Source — IUCN Palm Oil Task Force 2018.

Small-batch / Artisan

general

What brands imply

Handmade in tiny, careful quantities.

What it actually means

No definition of ‘small’ or ‘artisan’. Large manufacturers use these terms freely to imply craft and care.

Source — No regulatory standard.
