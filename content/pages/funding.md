A platform that examines an industry has to be able to say who pays for it. This page is that statement.

## What we accept

**Reader contributions.** Monthly supporting memberships and one-off contributions from individuals. This is the primary intended source and the one we would prefer to depend on entirely.

**Educational licensing.** Schools, universities, NGOs, and newsrooms licensing the curriculum for teaching and internal training. Licensees receive materials; they receive no influence over content.

**Commissioned research.** Scoped literature and regulation reviews for policy bodies, funders, and newsrooms. The funder is named on publication. The commissioning agreement gives the funder no right of approval over findings, and results are published openly unless the commission is genuinely commercially confidential — in which case we say a confidential commission was undertaken and name the sector.

**Grants** from foundations with no cosmetics-industry interest, disclosed on receipt.

## What we do not accept

**Cosmetics manufacturers, retailers, and ingredient suppliers.** No sponsorship, advertising, product placement, gifted products, paid partnership, or commissioned work of any kind, at any tier of the supply chain.

**Certification bodies.** We assess certification schemes. Taking their money would make that assessment worthless.

**Contract research organisations**, whether or not they conduct animal testing.

**Advertising**, of any kind, from anyone. There are no ad slots on this platform and no plans to introduce them.

**Affiliate revenue.** No affiliate links, referral codes, or commission arrangements. This is why we do not recommend products: a recommendation is worth more when it cannot be monetised, and we would rather forgo the revenue than have readers wonder.

## Why this matters more than usual here

The most common way an ethics platform loses its value is not corruption. It is the slow accumulation of relationships that make certain findings awkward.

A platform funded by brands would find it difficult to publish that a widely certified brand's parent company sells in markets requiring animal testing. A platform funded by a certification body would find it difficult to publish a comparison in which that body's scheme is documentation-based rather than audited.

We have published both. The funding structure is what makes that straightforward rather than difficult, and it is the reason to keep it restrictive even when the restriction is expensive.

## Transparency

We publish an annual funding report setting out total income by category, the number of supporting members, any commissioned work undertaken with the commissioning party named, and any offer declined that a reader might reasonably want to know about.

Supporters may be named in the report if they wish; the default is not to name.
