"Tested on animals" describes a category, not a procedure. Inside it sit a dozen distinct tests, developed at different times, asking different questions, with different degrees of replaceability. Every serious regulatory instrument in this field is written in terms of those distinctions, which is why reading one without them is close to impossible.

## The organising concept: endpoints

An **endpoint** is the specific biological effect a test is designed to detect. Toxicology is organised around endpoints, and so is the law.

This is why a statement like *this country has banned animal testing* is rarely a complete description. The EU marketing ban took effect for most endpoints in March 2009 and for three specific endpoints — repeated-dose toxicity, reproductive toxicity, and toxicokinetics — only in March 2013. Those four years were not administrative delay. They existed because replacements for those three endpoints were less developed, and the deferral was written into the regulation on that basis.

Endpoints are the unit in which this subject is legislated. Learn them and the instruments become readable.

## Local effects

These concern what happens where the substance touches. They are the shortest in duration and, not coincidentally, the most completely replaced.

**Skin irritation and corrosion.** Does the substance inflame skin reversibly, or destroy it irreversibly? Historically assessed by applying it to shaved rabbit skin under occlusion. Now assessed in reconstructed human epidermis under OECD Test Guidelines 431 and 439.

**Eye irritation and serious eye damage.** Does the substance damage the eye, and does that damage resolve? The Draize eye test is the procedure that made rabbits the emblem of this subject. Now addressed by TG 492 using reconstructed human corneal epithelium, alongside methods using abattoir-derived tissue.

**Skin sensitisation.** Does repeated contact induce an allergy? Historically the guinea pig maximisation test and later the murine local lymph node assay. Now fully addressed without animals by combining assays that each measure one step in the known biological pathway, under TG 442C, 442D, 442E, and the defined approaches of TG 497.

## Systemic effects

These concern what happens after a substance is absorbed and distributed through the body. They are harder to replace, and the difficulty is structural rather than a matter of insufficient effort.

**Acute systemic toxicity.** What single dose causes serious harm? The historical measure was the LD50 — the dose lethal to half of an exposed group. It is largely obsolete for cosmetics, and its decline is one of the clearest successes of the reduction agenda.

**Repeated-dose toxicity.** What happens under sustained exposure over weeks or months? Assessed in 28- or 90-day rodent studies. This is the endpoint with no single validated replacement, addressed instead by the Next Generation Risk Assessment framework.

**Reproductive and developmental toxicity.** Does the substance impair fertility or harm a developing foetus? Historically multi-generation rodent studies. Partially addressed by in vitro and computational approaches; not fully replaced.

**Toxicokinetics.** How is the substance absorbed, distributed, metabolised, and excreted? Not a harm question but a movement question — and the answer is what makes it possible to relate a cell-culture concentration to a real-world exposure. Now approached with physiologically based kinetic modelling.

**Carcinogenicity.** Does long-term exposure cause cancer? Historically two-year rodent bioassays. For cosmetic ingredients this endpoint is generally addressed by ensuring a substance is not genotoxic, rather than by running the bioassay.

## Genotoxicity: the special case

Genotoxicity — damage to DNA — occupies an unusual position worth understanding, because it demonstrates how a prohibition reshapes practice rather than merely restricting it.

The standard approach in other sectors is a battery of in vitro tests, with any positive result followed up in an animal study to determine whether the effect occurs in a whole organism. In vitro genotoxicity tests have a well-documented false-positive rate, so the follow-up matters.

For cosmetics in the EU, that follow-up is unavailable: the animal study is prohibited. The consequence is that a positive in vitro result cannot be resolved, and the ingredient is generally abandoned. The regime is therefore more conservative than the one it replaced — potentially useful ingredients are discarded on evidence that might not have survived scrutiny.

This is a real cost, and it is worth acknowledging rather than eliding. It is also an argument for investing in better in vitro methods with lower false-positive rates, which is precisely what has happened.

## Reading an exemption

With endpoints in hand, exemptions become legible. When an instrument carves out an exception, look for which of these it turns on:

**Which endpoint** — the EU's staged implementation is entirely an endpoint story.

**Which purpose** — cosmetic safety, or worker and environmental safety under chemicals law. The same test, differently motivated, is differently regulated.

**Which category** — China's special versus general cosmetics; Australia's chemicals used solely for cosmetics versus those with other uses.

**Which date** — data generated before an effective date almost always remains usable.

An exemption always turns on at least one of these four. Identifying which is the fastest way to understand what a law actually does.
