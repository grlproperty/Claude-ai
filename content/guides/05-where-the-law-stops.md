By this point the pattern should be visible. Every prohibition examined in this curriculum shares the same four gaps, in different proportions. This module names them, so that any new instrument can be assessed against them quickly.

## Gap one: the chemicals-law gap

The largest and the most consistent.

Cosmetics law asks whether a substance is safe on skin. Chemicals law asks whether it is safe to manufacture, transport, and release. These are different questions, answered under different statutes, and cosmetics prohibitions do not override chemicals obligations.

Present in the EU, in the UK, and — via exemptions for ingredients with substantial non-cosmetic uses — in most US state bans.

**Closeable by legislation?** In principle, yes: a legislature could exempt cosmetic ingredients from animal-data requirements under chemicals law. It has not happened anywhere, because doing so requires accepting that worker and environmental risk will be characterised by other means, and regulators have not yet been persuaded that those means are adequate for every endpoint. So the gap is legislatively closeable but scientifically gated.

## Gap two: the historical-data gap

Every prohibition applies from an effective date. Data generated before it remains usable indefinitely.

This is not a drafting oversight. Retroactive invalidation would remove the safety basis for ingredients in use for decades, and would require either withdrawing them or re-testing them by other means at enormous expense. No jurisdiction has attempted it.

**Closeable?** Effectively no, and it matters less over time. Its practical effect is that the ingredients most widely used today rest on animal data that will never be regenerated. That is a permanent feature of the landscape rather than a live policy question.

## Gap three: the category gap

Prohibitions cover defined categories, and things outside those categories are unaffected.

China's special cosmetics. Australia's chemicals with non-cosmetic uses. Everywhere: products regulated as drugs rather than cosmetics, where animal testing requirements have historically been explicit. A sunscreen is a cosmetic in Europe and an over-the-counter drug in the United States, and the applicable rules differ accordingly.

**Closeable?** Category by category, yes — this is ordinary legislative work, and it is where most incremental progress happens. The 2021 Chinese reform was exactly this: a category boundary moved.

## Gap four: the enforcement gap

A prohibition with no verification mechanism relies on self-reporting.

Most cosmetics regimes are post-market and document-based. A company holds a dossier and produces it on inspection. Whether inspections occur at a rate sufficient to detect non-compliance is an empirical question, and in most jurisdictions the answer is not published.

Claims regulation compounds this. Where *cruelty-free* has no legal definition, an inaccurate claim is an advertising matter enforced on complaint. Canada's 2023 reform — making false cruelty-free claims a specific offence — is the clearest attempt to close this gap so far, and it is worth watching as a natural experiment.

**Closeable?** Yes, by resourcing enforcement and by defining terms in law. Both are within ordinary legislative and administrative competence. Neither is glamorous, which is part of why they lag.

## What an accurate summary sounds like

Putting it together, a defensible statement of the global position:

More than forty jurisdictions restrict animal testing for cosmetics in some form. The European Union operates the most comprehensive regime, prohibiting both testing and the marketing of products relying on new animal data. That regime does not prevent animal testing of cosmetic ingredients conducted under chemicals law for worker and environmental safety.

China removed mandatory pre-market animal testing for imported general cosmetics in 2021, subject to certification and safety-assessment conditions, while retaining requirements for special cosmetics and the authority to conduct post-market testing.

The United States has no federal prohibition; a growing number of states prohibit the sale of cosmetics developed using new animal testing, with exemptions that reproduce the chemicals-law gap.

For most toxicological endpoints, validated non-animal methods exist and are adopted as OECD Test Guidelines. For repeated-dose systemic toxicity and full reproductive toxicity, no single validated replacement exists; framework approaches are in use and developing.

That paragraph is longer than *animal testing is banned in Europe*. It is also true, and it is the version that survives contact with someone who knows the subject.

## What follows from knowing this

Three practical consequences.

**In argument**, the accurate version is stronger. Overstatement is the most common way a well-founded position is discredited, and this field offers unusually many opportunities for it.

**In advocacy**, the gaps point at specific targets: chemicals-law exemption for cosmetic ingredients, validation funding, statutory definition of claims, enforcement resourcing. These are more tractable than a general demand, because each has an identifiable decision-maker.

**In purchasing**, understanding the gaps means understanding what a certification can and cannot promise — and treating it as a well-evidenced judgement rather than a guarantee.
