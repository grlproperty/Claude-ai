The previous modules were structural. This one is practical: a method you can apply while standing in a shop.

It will not always produce a clean answer, because the information required is sometimes not public. Knowing *that* is itself useful — it tells you when the honest conclusion is "this brand has not disclosed enough to judge", which is a legitimate finding rather than a failure.

## Step one: identify what kind of claim it is

Three categories, distinguishable at a glance.

**A certification mark** — the Leaping Bunny logo, a PETA bunny logo, a recognised vegan trademark. These are registered marks with published criteria. You can look up what they require.

**An uncertified claim in words** — *not tested on animals*, *cruelty-free*, *against animal testing*. No verification, though not necessarily untrue.

**No claim at all.** More common than either. It carries no information in itself: the absence of a claim is not evidence of testing, only of silence.

## Step two: the four questions

For any claim, certified or not:

**Does it cover ingredients or only the finished product?** Testing overwhelmingly happens at ingredient level. A finished-product claim is the most common construction that is literally true and materially incomplete.

**Does it extend to suppliers and contract laboratories?** A company that commissions testing rather than performing it has not stopped testing.

**Is there a fixed cut-off date?** A specific date after which no testing has occurred anywhere in the supply chain. Auditable, and it survives reformulation. Present-tense claims say nothing about next year.

**Does the company sell where an authority may require testing?** Not answerable from packaging. It is answerable from the brand's own market list and its certification scope.

A certification mark answers the first three by reference to its published criteria. An uncertified claim answers none of them unless the company has published a policy — and if it has, that policy is the document to read, not the bottle.

## Step three: separate vegan from cruelty-free

The most common error on a shelf, and the easiest to fix once seen.

**Vegan** is a claim about ingredients: nothing animal-derived in the formulation. No beeswax, no lanolin, no carmine, no keratin.

**Cruelty-free** is a claim about method: how the formulation's safety was assessed.

They are independent. A product can be vegan and animal-tested. It can be certified cruelty-free and contain honey. Certification schemes that publish both categories separately — as PETA's does — are being precise, not redundant.

## Step four: find the owner

Packaging rarely names the parent company. A search of the brand name with the word *owned by* usually resolves it in under a minute.

What you do with the answer is a judgement this platform does not make for you. A certified brand under a parent that sells elsewhere in markets requiring testing presents a genuine dilemma, and both positions on it are held in good faith:

One view holds that the certification is what it says — this brand meets these criteria — and that supporting compliant brands rewards compliance and creates internal commercial pressure at group level.

The other holds that revenue is fungible, that group profit is group profit, and that purchasing from a certified subsidiary supports a corporate entity whose overall practice is not certified.

Neither is unreasonable. The point of this step is that you cannot hold either position without the ownership information, and it is trivially obtainable.

## What packaging cannot tell you

Be clear about the limits, because knowing them prevents false confidence:

Whether an ingredient was animal-tested by a supplier before the brand existed.

Whether testing occurred under chemicals law for worker or environmental safety rather than under cosmetics law.

Whether a regulator has conducted post-market testing on the product.

Whether the parent company's other brands operate to the same standard.

These are not answerable at the shelf. Some are answerable from a brand's published policy; some are not answerable at all with public information. A company that publishes a detailed policy addressing supply chain, cut-off date, and market presence has told you a great deal — and the willingness to publish is itself informative.

## The two-minute method

In practice:

Look for a certification mark. If present, you know what its criteria require.

If absent, look for a claim. If a claim is present, treat it as an assertion to verify rather than as information.

Search the brand plus *animal testing policy*. A brand serious about the commitment publishes one, and it will address the four questions.

Search the brand plus *owned by*. Decide what the answer means to you.

Two minutes, and the result is a defensible judgement rather than a guess. That is the whole objective — not certainty, which is unavailable, but a judgement you can explain.

## Check yourself

What is the difference between a claim that is false and a claim that is unverifiable — and why does it matter which one you are looking at?

Why does a fixed cut-off date make a claim more robust than a present-tense statement?

Why is the parent-company question an ethical judgement rather than a factual one?
