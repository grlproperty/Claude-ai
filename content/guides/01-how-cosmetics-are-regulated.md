Every argument about animal testing in beauty runs through a regulatory system, and most arguments go wrong because the participants are describing different systems without noticing. This module establishes the architecture. It is deliberately dry. Everything else in the curriculum depends on it.

## What counts as a cosmetic

A cosmetic is defined by what it claims to do, not by what it contains.

In the European Union, Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009 defines a cosmetic product as a substance or mixture intended to be placed in contact with the external parts of the human body — or with teeth and oral mucous membranes — exclusively or mainly to clean, perfume, change appearance, protect, keep in good condition, or correct body odours.

In the United States, the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act defines cosmetics as articles intended to be applied to the human body for cleansing, beautifying, promoting attractiveness, or altering the appearance.

The word doing the work in both definitions is *intended*. A cream that moisturises is a cosmetic. The same cream, sold with a claim that it treats eczema, is making a therapeutic claim and may be a drug — subject to an entirely different and far more demanding regime, in which animal testing requirements have historically been explicit rather than absent.

This is why the boundary matters. Sunscreens illustrate it well: regulated as cosmetics in the EU, as over-the-counter drugs in the US. The same bottle sits in different regulatory universes depending on where it is sold.

## Who establishes safety

Here the systems diverge sharply, and the divergence explains a great deal.

**The European Union** requires a Responsible Person for each product placed on the market, a Product Information File, and a Cosmetic Product Safety Report prepared by a qualified safety assessor. There is no pre-market approval by a regulator: the company assembles the dossier, takes responsibility for it, and must produce it on demand. Enforcement is national and inspection-based.

**The United States**, following MoCRA in 2022, requires facility registration, product listing, and adequate substantiation of safety, with FDA holding recall authority. There is still no pre-market approval for most cosmetics. Colour additives are the significant exception; they require pre-market approval, and that regime is much older.

**China** operates the most interventionist model of the three. Under CSAR, special cosmetics require registration with the National Medical Products Administration — genuine pre-market approval — while general cosmetics require filing. This is the structural reason animal testing requirements have persisted longer there: a system that approves products before sale needs a defined evidentiary standard to approve them against, and animal test data was that standard.

## Pre-market and post-market

The distinction turns out to be the hinge on which the whole testing question swings.

**Pre-market** systems require evidence before a product may be sold. If the regulator specifies animal data as acceptable evidence, testing follows automatically.

**Post-market** systems place the obligation on the company to hold adequate evidence, and check afterwards. The company chooses the method. If the law forbids animal data, it chooses something else.

The EU's cosmetics ban works precisely because the system is post-market: prohibiting reliance on animal data does not leave a regulator without an approval standard, because there was no approval step to begin with. Removing a testing requirement from a pre-market system is a harder reform, because something has to take its place.

Understanding this is what makes the pace of change in different jurisdictions legible. It is not simply that some countries care more.

## Where testing enters

Animal testing enters a cosmetics regime at exactly two points.

**As a requirement**, when a pre-market approval system specifies animal data as the standard for demonstrating safety.

**As a choice**, when a post-market system requires adequate substantiation without specifying method, and the company selects animal testing — usually because it is the method with the longest regulatory track record, or because the ingredient is also regulated as an industrial chemical under a scheme that demands it.

The second is the harder problem, and it is where the remaining testing in Europe and the United States actually sits. Prohibiting a requirement is straightforward legislative work. Prohibiting a choice made under a different statute for a different purpose is not.
