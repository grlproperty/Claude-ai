There is a sentence that appears in almost every article written about cosmetics and animal testing: *the EU banned animal testing for cosmetics in 2013*. It is true. It is also, on its own, misleading enough to have shaped a decade of consumer misunderstanding.

The sentence describes one regulation. The practice it appears to describe is governed by two.

## Two instruments, one ingredient

Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009 governs cosmetic products in the European Union. Article 18 contains the prohibitions that made headlines: a ban on testing finished cosmetic products on animals, a ban on testing cosmetic ingredients on animals, and — crucially — a ban on *placing on the market* any cosmetic whose safety was established using animal data generated after the relevant date.

That last prohibition is what gives the regime its reach. A testing ban would only bind laboratories inside the EU; a company could test in a jurisdiction without one and import the result. The marketing ban closes that route. It is the single most consequential provision in cosmetics law anywhere in the world, and it is why the EU regime is treated as the global benchmark.

Regulation (EC) No 1907/2006 — REACH — governs chemicals. Not cosmetics: chemicals. Its concern is not whether a substance is safe on a consumer's face but whether it is safe for the worker who handles it in bulk, and what happens when it reaches a watercourse. It requires registrants to supply data sufficient to characterise those risks. For some substances, at some tonnages, for some endpoints, that data has to come from animals.

A substance can be within the scope of both. When it is, the two instruments give different answers, and cosmetics law does not override chemicals law.

## What the Board of Appeal decided

This was not a theoretical concern. In August 2020, the ECHA Board of Appeal ruled on two appeals brought by Symrise, a fragrance and ingredient manufacturer, against decisions requiring animal testing of substances used exclusively in cosmetics.

Symrise argued that the cosmetics regulation prohibited the testing. The Board held that the REACH obligations stood: where the concern being characterised is worker safety or environmental exposure rather than consumer safety, the cosmetics regulation's prohibitions do not displace them.

The practical consequence is precise, and it is worth stating carefully. A cosmetic ingredient may be animal-tested, lawfully, within the European Union, provided the testing is conducted to satisfy chemicals law rather than cosmetics law. The finished product containing it may then be sold as compliant with a regime that is universally described as a ban.

## Why this is not a scandal, and why it still matters

It would be easy to present this as concealment. It is not. Both regulations are public, the Board of Appeal decisions are published, and the tension between them has been debated openly in the European Parliament for years.

What it is, instead, is a structural gap — the kind that appears whenever two regulatory systems with different objectives are asked to govern the same molecule. Cosmetics law asks: is this safe to put on skin? Chemicals law asks: is this safe to manufacture, transport, and release? Those are different questions. They were answered by different directorates, at different times, with different evidentiary requirements.

The gap matters because of what consumers reasonably infer from the word *ban*. A shopper who has read that animal testing for cosmetics is prohibited in Europe will not assume that an ingredient in the bottle they are holding was tested on animals last year in a laboratory in the European Union. That inference is wrong, and nothing on the packaging corrects it.

## The Citizens' Initiative

In 2021 and 2022, a European Citizens' Initiative titled *Save Cruelty-Free Cosmetics — Commit to a Europe Without Animal Testing* gathered more than 1.2 million validated signatures across the required minimum of Member States. It made three demands: protect and strengthen the cosmetics testing ban, transform EU chemicals regulation, and modernise science in the EU by committing to a legislative proposal phasing out all animal testing.

The Commission responded in July 2023. It declined to propose an immediate legislative ban on animal testing for cosmetic ingredients under REACH, on the stated grounds that worker and environmental safety data requirements cannot presently be met by other means for every endpoint. It committed instead to a roadmap for phasing out animal testing in chemical safety assessment, and to reinforcing support for the development and validation of non-animal methods.

Whether a roadmap is an adequate answer to 1.2 million signatures is a political question, and this platform does not answer political questions. What can be stated as fact is that the gap identified by the initiative remains open, and that the Commission's response acknowledged it rather than denying it.

## What to take from this

Three things follow, and they generalise well beyond Europe.

First, the phrase *animal testing is banned* is almost never a complete description of a legal position. Bans are written endpoint by endpoint, category by category, and purpose by purpose. The interesting information is always in the exemptions.

Second, the purpose for which a test is conducted can determine its legality. The same experiment on the same substance may be prohibited when performed to establish cosmetic safety and required when performed to establish occupational safety.

Third, a compliance claim and an ethical claim are different claims. A company can be entirely compliant with the most stringent cosmetics regime in the world and still sell a product containing an animal-tested ingredient. It is not lying when it says so. The question of whether that satisfies the buyer is one the buyer has to answer, and they can only answer it if they know the structure.
