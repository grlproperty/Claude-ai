A shopper holding two bottles has a harder problem than they realise. Both say *not tested on animals*. One statement is backed by a fixed cut-off date, annual supplier declarations, and an audit programme. The other was written by a marketing department. Nothing on either bottle distinguishes them.

This is not a failure of individual companies. It is a consequence of the phrase being, in most jurisdictions, undefined in law.

## Where the words come from

Cosmetics regulation generally addresses claims through a general prohibition on misleading consumers rather than by defining specific terms. In the EU, Regulation (EU) No 655/2013 lays down common criteria for cosmetic claims — legal compliance, truthfulness, evidential support, honesty, fairness, and informed decision-making. It does not define *cruelty-free*.

The practical result is that the phrase means whatever the company applying it can defend if challenged, and challenges are rare. Canada is the significant recent exception: its December 2023 reform of the Food and Drugs Act made it an offence to make a false or misleading cruelty-free claim, which converts the question from an advertising-standards matter into a regulatory one.

## The four structural questions

Every meaningful distinction between claims reduces to four questions. They are worth memorising, because they work on any label in any market.

**Does the claim cover ingredients, or only the finished product?**

This is the most common and most consequential ambiguity. Testing overwhelmingly occurs at ingredient level — a novel preservative, a new UV filter, a fragrance component. A company that has never tested a finished formulation on an animal, and never would, may nonetheless be using ingredients that were animal-tested by the supplier who developed them. A finished-product claim is literally true and materially incomplete.

**Does it extend to suppliers and contract laboratories?**

A company that conducts no testing itself but commissions a contract research organisation to conduct it has not stopped testing. Robust schemes require declarations covering the entire supply chain, not just the certificate holder's own premises.

**Is there a fixed cut-off date?**

This is the feature that most sharply separates the schemes. A fixed cut-off date is a specific date after which the company commits that no animal testing has occurred anywhere in its supply chain. It is auditable, and it survives reformulation: a company cannot certify a product, later commission testing on a reformulated ingredient, and keep the mark.

A rolling or unstated cut-off means the claim describes the present tense only. It says nothing about what happens next year.

**Does the company sell where an authority may require testing?**

This is where the China question, the parent-company question, and the ethical judgement all converge — and it is the one that cannot be resolved by reading the label at all.

## What each scheme actually verifies

**Leaping Bunny**, operated by Cruelty Free International and the Coalition for Consumer Information on Cosmetics, is the most demanding of the widely used schemes. It requires a fixed cut-off date, written declarations from every ingredient supplier, a documented supplier monitoring system, annual recommitment, and openness to independent audit. Its scope covers the company, its suppliers, and any third party acting on its behalf.

**PETA's Beauty Without Bunnies** operates on a signed statement of assurance from the company covering itself and its suppliers, and publishes two categories — animal test-free, and animal test-free and vegan. It is documentation-based rather than audit-based. That is a real difference in evidential weight; it is not a reason to dismiss the programme, which lists a far larger number of companies and has driven a great deal of change simply by making the commitment visible.

**Vegan trademarks** answer a different question altogether, and the confusion between the two is the most common error on a shelf. Vegan is a claim about what is in the formulation. Cruelty-free is a claim about how the formulation's safety was assessed. A product can be vegan and animal-tested; it can be cruelty-free and contain beeswax. Neither implies the other.

**Uncertified claims** verify nothing that anyone outside the company has checked. This does not make them false — many are entirely accurate. It makes them unverifiable, which is a different failing and, for a consumer trying to choose, an equally disabling one.

## The parent-company question

A brand may hold a rigorous certification while its parent company owns other brands that sell in markets requiring animal testing. The certification is accurate: the certified brand meets the criteria. The ethical question — whether purchasing from a certified subsidiary meaningfully supports or undermines the practice at group level — is genuinely contested, and this platform does not resolve it.

What we do insist on is that the question cannot be answered without the ownership information, and that ownership is frequently not disclosed anywhere on the packaging. If a reader wants to make that judgement, the first step is finding out who owns the brand. It is usually a two-minute search, and it is usually the piece of information that changes the answer.

## A closing note on proportion

It is tempting to read all of this as an argument that certifications are worthless. It is the opposite argument.

Certification schemes are the only mechanism currently converting a vague marketing phrase into something checkable. Their limitations are real and worth understanding precisely — that is what this entry is for. But a certified claim with a fixed cut-off date and supplier declarations is not merely better than an uncertified one; it is a different kind of statement altogether. One is a promise with a verification mechanism attached. The other is a sentence.
