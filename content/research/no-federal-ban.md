The United States is frequently listed alongside the European Union in summaries of countries that have restricted cosmetic animal testing. At federal level this is incorrect, and the way the correction works is more interesting than the error.

## The federal position

The Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act does not require animal testing for cosmetics and does not prohibit it. The Food and Drug Administration has long stated that it does not specifically require the use of animals in testing cosmetics for safety, while holding manufacturers responsible for substantiating the safety of their products by whatever means they select. The choice of method rests with the company.

Two recent developments are routinely misread as changing this.

The **Modernization of Cosmetics Regulation Act of 2022** (MoCRA) was the most significant expansion of federal cosmetics authority in more than eighty years. It introduced mandatory facility registration, product listing, safety substantiation requirements, adverse event reporting, and recall authority. It did not restrict animal testing.

The **FDA Modernization Act 2.0**, enacted at the same time, removed the statutory requirement for animal testing in new drug applications, allowing sponsors to use alternatives including cell-based assays and computational models. It concerns drugs. It has no application to cosmetics, and the conflation of the two is the most common factual error in coverage of this subject.

The **Humane Cosmetics Act** has been introduced in successive Congresses and has not been enacted.

## Where the restrictions actually are

Federal inaction pushed the question to the states, and the mechanism they chose is the reason it worked.

State legislation in this area does not generally prohibit testing. It prohibits *selling* cosmetics developed or manufactured using animal testing conducted after a stated effective date. California's SB 1249, the California Cruelty-Free Cosmetics Act, established the template when it took effect on 1 January 2020.

This structure matters enormously. A testing ban in one state would relocate a laboratory. A sales ban in a state representing a large share of national consumption changes what is worth manufacturing at all. Few companies maintain separate formulations and separate safety dossiers for individual states. In practice, compliance is achieved nationally.

By the mid-2020s, states with such laws in force included California, Illinois, Nevada, Hawaii, Louisiana, Maine, Maryland, New Jersey, Virginia, New York, Oregon, and Washington. Together they account for a substantial fraction of the US consumer market — enough that the commercial calculation for a national brand is largely settled even though federal law is silent.

## The exemptions

State bans are narrower than their headlines. Common carve-outs include:

Testing conducted to comply with a requirement of a foreign regulatory authority, where the resulting product is not sold in the state in reliance on that data.

Testing conducted for an ingredient with a substantial non-cosmetic use, where the testing was required by a federal or state agency under a different regulatory scheme.

Data generated before the effective date, which remains usable indefinitely.

Testing required by a federal agency for a purpose other than cosmetic safety.

The second and fourth exemptions reproduce, in miniature, exactly the chemicals-law gap that runs through the European regime. An ingredient used in both cosmetics and industrial applications can be animal-tested under the authority of a different agency for a different purpose, and a cosmetic containing it can still be sold.

## Reading a US claim

The federal silence has a specific consequence for labels. Because no federal instrument defines *cruelty-free*, the phrase is governed by general prohibitions on deceptive advertising — which are enforced reactively, on complaint, rather than through pre-market review.

Canada's 2023 reform is the useful contrast. By making a false cruelty-free claim a specific offence under the Food and Drugs Act, Canada converted the question from an advertising dispute into a regulatory one with a named enforcer. The United States has not taken that step.

For a reader in the US, this means the certification schemes are doing work that in other jurisdictions is partly done by law. That raises the stakes on understanding what each scheme verifies — which is the subject of a separate entry in this archive.
