For most of the last decade, the position was simple enough to summarise in a sentence: a cosmetics brand could be certified cruelty-free, or it could sell in mainland China, but not both. Chinese law required pre-market animal testing for imported cosmetics. Entering that market meant abandoning the claim.

On 1 May 2021 that changed — partially, conditionally, and for one category of product. The reform is real and significant. It is also the single most over-summarised development in this field, and the gap between what happened and what is reported has become a reliable source of consumer error.

## The instrument

The Cosmetic Supervision and Administration Regulation — CSAR, State Council Order No. 727 — replaced a regulatory framework that had been in place since 1989. It came into force on 1 January 2021, with the testing provisions and supporting implementing rules taking effect on 1 May.

CSAR divides cosmetics into two classes, and everything that follows depends on which class a product falls into.

**Special cosmetics** are products for hair dyeing, hair perming, freckle-removing and whitening, sun protection, and anti-hair-loss, plus any product making a new efficacy claim. These remain subject to registration with the National Medical Products Administration and to the associated testing requirements.

**General cosmetics** are everything else — the large majority of cleansers, moisturisers, and colour cosmetics. These are filed rather than registered, and it is here that the exemption applies.

## The exemption, stated precisely

An imported general cosmetic may be exempted from pre-market animal testing where two conditions are satisfied:

The manufacturer holds a Good Manufacturing Practice certificate, or equivalent, issued by the competent regulatory authority of the country in which the product is produced. This is a certificate about the factory, issued by a government, not a self-declaration.

The applicant submits a product safety assessment demonstrating the product's safety, in the form the NMPA requires.

Both conditions must be met. Neither is automatic, and the first has proved to be the binding constraint in practice, because not every jurisdiction issues cosmetic GMP certificates in a form the NMPA accepts, and not every manufacturer can obtain one.

## What was not changed

Four things survived the reform, and each of them matters.

**Special cosmetics still require testing.** A brand whose range includes a sunscreen or a whitening product is in a different position from one whose range does not — even if every other product it sells qualifies for the exemption.

**Products for infants and children are excluded from the exemption**, as are products using ingredients still within their statutory monitoring period. These carve-outs are narrow but real.

**Post-market testing authority is unaffected.** Regulators retain the power to sample and test products already on sale, and that testing may involve animals. A brand cannot control whether this occurs, and its occurrence does not indicate any failure by the brand.

**Domestic manufacture was always treated differently.** Domestically produced non-special-use cosmetics have been exempt from mandatory pre-market animal testing since 2014. Some brands entered the market by manufacturing locally long before 2021, which is why the picture was never quite as binary as the pre-2021 summary suggested.

## How certification bodies responded

The response was not uniform, and the divergence is instructive.

Cruelty Free International's Leaping Bunny programme maintains that the possibility of post-market testing and the conditionality of the exemption mean that market presence requires case-by-case assessment rather than blanket acceptance. PETA's Beauty Without Bunnies programme took a position that treats the 2021 changes as making compliant sale possible for qualifying products.

Neither position is unreasonable. They reflect a genuine judgement call about how much residual risk a certification should tolerate — and they are a useful reminder that certifications encode ethical judgements, not just facts. Two schemes reading the same regulation reached different answers because they were answering slightly different questions.

## The question to ask

If a brand tells you it is now able to sell in China without animal testing, the claim may well be accurate. It becomes verifiable when you know three things:

Which categories does it sell there — does the range include special cosmetics?

Does it hold the required GMP certification, issued by which authority?

Does its cruelty-free certification, if any, extend to its Chinese-market products specifically, or only to those sold elsewhere?

A brand that can answer all three is being straightforward with you. A brand that responds only with the general statement that China no longer requires animal testing has told you something that is true in the abstract and unverified in its own case.
