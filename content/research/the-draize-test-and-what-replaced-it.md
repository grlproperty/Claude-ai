In 1944, John Draize and colleagues at the United States Food and Drug Administration published a scoring system for assessing the irritancy of substances applied to the eye and skin of rabbits. The procedure was straightforward: apply the test substance to the eye of a restrained animal, or to shaved skin, and score the resulting damage over a period of days against a defined scale.

It was designed to solve a real problem. Cosmetics were causing injuries, there was no systematic way to assess a new formulation before sale, and a standardised, reproducible test allowed regulators to compare products. It worked, in the sense that it produced numbers that could be compared.

Eighty years later it is the reason a rabbit is the visual shorthand for this entire subject. It is also, by contemporary standards, a poor test — and understanding why is more useful than understanding that it was cruel.

## Why the Draize test was replaced

The ethical case is obvious and has been made well by others. The scientific case is less discussed and, for anyone arguing about regulation, more useful.

**Species relevance.** The rabbit eye differs from the human eye in tear production, blinking rate, corneal thickness, and pH buffering. A substance's behaviour in one is an imperfect predictor of its behaviour in the other.

**Reproducibility.** Scoring is performed by observation against a descriptive scale. Inter-laboratory and inter-observer variability in Draize results has been documented repeatedly, and it is substantial. A test that gives different answers in different hands is a weak foundation for a safety decision.

**Resolution.** The test returns a severity score. It does not identify the mechanism of injury, which means it cannot support inference about related substances, and every new molecule starts from zero.

Modern replacements were not adopted merely because they spare animals. They were adopted because they are more reproducible, use human tissue, and — in the case of skin sensitisation — resolve the biology into discrete measurable events rather than a single opaque outcome.

## What replaced it, endpoint by endpoint

**Skin corrosion and irritation.** OECD Test Guidelines 431 and 439 use reconstructed human epidermis: a three-dimensional tissue model grown from donated human keratinocytes and differentiated until it resembles living epidermis, complete with a stratum corneum. The substance is applied, and cell viability is measured. A corrosive kills the tissue quickly; an irritant reduces viability below a defined threshold; a non-irritant does not. The tissue is human, the readout is quantitative, and the result is reproducible across laboratories.

**Serious eye damage and eye irritation.** OECD TG 492 applies the same principle to reconstructed human cornea-like epithelium. Two further guidelines — TG 437 and TG 438, using bovine corneas and isolated chicken eyes — use tissue recovered from animals already slaughtered for food. These reduce animal use substantially without eliminating it, and the distinction is worth preserving rather than blurring: a method using abattoir tissue is a reduction method, not a replacement.

**Skin sensitisation.** This is the endpoint that best demonstrates what modern regulatory toxicology actually looks like, and it deserves the detail.

Allergic contact dermatitis develops through a known sequence: the substance binds to skin proteins, keratinocytes respond, dendritic cells activate, T-cells proliferate. This sequence is formalised as an adverse outcome pathway. Rather than using one animal test as a proxy for the whole chain, three test guidelines each measure one key event — TG 442C for protein binding, TG 442D for keratinocyte activation, TG 442E for dendritic cell activation. TG 497 then specifies *defined approaches*: fixed, published rules for combining those results into a regulatory classification.

The outcome is a decision reached without animals, from measurements of the actual biological mechanism, using a data interpretation procedure written down in advance. That last feature — a fixed procedure rather than expert judgement applied case by case — is what makes it regulatorily robust.

**Phototoxicity.** TG 432, the 3T3 neutral red uptake assay, has been accepted in the EU since 2000. Cells are exposed with and without controlled simulated sunlight; a phototoxic substance is markedly more cytotoxic under irradiation. It is one of the oldest full replacements and among the most reliable.

**Dermal absorption.** TG 428 mounts excised human or porcine skin in a diffusion cell and measures passage directly. Human skin from surgical procedures gives the most relevant answer obtainable by any method, animal tests included.

## What has not been replaced

Honesty about this is the point at which advocacy either earns credibility or loses it.

Repeated-dose systemic toxicity — the harm that emerges from exposure over weeks or months rather than from a single contact — has no single validated replacement. Neither does reproductive and developmental toxicity in full.

The response is not one test but a framework: Next Generation Risk Assessment. An internal exposure is estimated with a physiologically based kinetic model; a point of departure is derived from in vitro bioactivity assays across a wide range of biological targets; the two are compared to establish whether a margin of safety exists. This has been applied successfully in specific cosmetic safety assessments and published as case studies. It is not yet a drop-in replacement accepted across all regulatory sectors for all substances.

Anyone who tells you this problem is fully solved is overstating it. Anyone who tells you it is intractable is understating six decades of progress. Both statements damage the argument they are meant to support.

## Why the science matters to the politics

The most common objection to extending testing bans is that safety would suffer. The most effective answer is not that animal welfare outweighs consumer safety. It is that for most endpoints the non-animal method is the better test — more reproducible, conducted in human tissue, mechanistically informative — and that the remaining gap is narrower and better characterised than the objection assumes.

That argument requires knowing which endpoints are solved and which are not. Which is why the honest version, including the part about repeated-dose toxicity, is also the more persuasive one.
