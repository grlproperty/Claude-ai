# A Body That Could Not Agree

**Working title.** The site's own thesis sentence, which is the strongest line
FERAL FEMME has and should not be spent on a subtitle.

**One-line pitch.** Every industry examined here makes its money somewhere on a
body that was never asked — and the machinery built to reassure us about that is
more interesting, and more documented, than the cruelty itself.

**Length.** 75,000–85,000 words. Fourteen chapters across four parts, plus a
reference appendix drawn from the datasets.

**Who it is for.** The reader who already suspects the labels are lying and
wants to know exactly how, with sources. Not a converting text. The people who
need converting will not buy a book about supply chains.

---

## Why this book and not another one

The shelf is already full of books that establish that fast fashion is bad,
factory farming is bad, and marketing manipulates people. This is not that book
and cannot compete with those, most of which are a decade old and better
resourced.

What does not exist is a book about **the reassurance apparatus** — the
certifications, the social audits, the offset registries, the ESG scores, the
"conscious" sub-lines — treated as an industry in its own right, with its own
economics and its own incentives, documented case by case. That apparatus is
the actual subject FERAL FEMME has been assembling a database about for months.
Nobody else has 22 certification scopes and 21 material profiles written up
with what each one *excludes*.

The two-part structure of that argument — the bodies, then the machinery that
lets us not look at them — is the book. Part Three is the reason to write it.

---

## The organising claim

Three claims, in order, each doing work the next one depends on:

1. **Consent is the common thread, not sentiment.** Animals and women appear
   together here not as a metaphorical pairing but because the same structural
   fact is true of both: an industry is extracting value from a body whose
   agreement was never obtainable or never sought. Naming that plainly is what
   lets the book move between a broiler shed and a garment floor without
   sliding into comparison.

2. **The cruelty is rarely hidden. It is renamed.** Tradition, testing,
   farming, research, wellness, self-care. The vocabulary is the mechanism, not
   the cover story — which is why a book about words can be a book about bodies.

3. **The reassurance industry is the load-bearing structure.** Certification,
   audit and offset schemes are what make the arrangement survivable for the
   consumer, the brand and the regulator simultaneously. They are not failures
   of a good system; they are a functioning system doing what it was built to
   do.

## Part One — The Vocabulary

*The words come first because everything after them is described in them.*

### 1. A body that could not agree
The thesis, stated plainly and defended against the obvious objection: that
putting animals and women in one sentence diminishes both. It does not, and the
chapter says why — the claim is about the structure of the transaction, not the
moral weight of the subject. Establishes the method: public record, cited,
correctable, and the correction published.

*Draws on:* the site's positioning and editorial standards.

### 2. Thirty-six words
The greenwashing vocabulary as machinery. "Natural" has no legal definition in
cosmetics or food labelling in most jurisdictions. "Chemical-free" cannot be
substantiated because water is a chemical. "Cage-free" is a statement about
cages and nothing else. Each word is doing a specific job, and the jobs are
classifiable.

*Draws on:* `greenwashing.json` (36 terms); field notes 10, 12, 16.

### 3. The baseline nobody states
On comparatives. "Greener", "better for the planet", "more sustainable" — than
what? The most common single finding in regulatory casework is a comparative
claim with no comparator. A short, almost technical chapter, and the one that
gives the reader a tool they will use in a supermarket that week.

*Draws on:* the CMA Green Claims Code casework; field note 6.

## Part Two — The Bodies

*Four chapters, four specific bodies, no summarising.*

### 4. Seventy-five million people
The garment supply chain. Approximately 75 million people make the world's
clothes; roughly 80% are women; most are paid below what their own governments
define as a living wage. The chapter resists the composite-worker device and
stays with what is actually documented: wage data, the Asia Floor Wage
calculations, the Sumangali scheme in Tamil Nadu.

*Draws on:* field note 2; `library.json` (Clean Clothes, SOMO, Fair Wear).

### 5. The quiet arithmetic
Industrial animal agriculture, told through numbers that do not move much from
year to year — which is the point, and the reason the chapter is arithmetic
rather than atrocity. Includes the honest problem of scale insensitivity: why a
number this large stops functioning as information.

*Draws on:* field notes 4, 8; `materials.json`.

### 6. The ones we decided not to count
Fish, and the conversation that has not happened. The clearest case in the book
of a body excluded from consideration by category rather than by argument, which
makes it the best available test of whether the reader's principle is a
principle or a preference.

*Draws on:* field note 14.

### 7. The industry that sells the doubt it makes
Beauty, valued by McKinsey at roughly $446 billion in 2023, and the documented
evidence on advertising exposure and body image — particularly for younger
women and girls. The one chapter where the non-consenting body is the reader's
own, which is where the book stops being about other people.

*Draws on:* field notes 1, 18, 11; `record.json` (beauty findings).

## Part Three — The Machinery of Reassurance

*The centre of the book. This is the part nobody else has written.*

### 8. Reading an audit that was never going to find anything
**Sample chapter written — see `CHAPTER-08.md`.** The social audit industry, and
the two cases that define it: Ali Enterprises in Karachi, certified SA8000 by
RINA three weeks before a fire that killed more than 250 people; and Rana Plaza,
where two factories had been audited against the BSCI code and building safety
was outside the scope of that code by design. The argument is not that the
audits failed. It is that they did what they were built to do, and that the
certificate never claimed what its readers believed it claimed.

### 9. What the logo does not cover
**Written — see `CHAPTER-09.md`.** Twenty-two certification schemes, and the gap
between what each verifies and what its logo implies.

The chapter was outlined as a scheme-by-scheme walk. Writing it turned up
something better: the gaps have **shapes**, six of them, and naming the shape
lets a reader predict where a scheme they have never seen will turn out to be
narrower than it looks. *The adjacent virtue* (Leaping Bunny certifies testing,
not ingredients). *The mixing* (mass balance and credits, where the certified
material and the sold material need never have met). *The threshold* (GRS needs
20% recycled content to carry the logo). *The wrong subject* (B Corp certifies
a company, ROC certifies a farm, neither certifies your purchase). *The
evidence* (PETA's base mark is a signed pledge; Leaping Bunny is audited). And
*the gap that cannot be closed* — welfare has always meant the conditions of a
life, not the absence of an ending.

*Draws on:* `certifications.json` (all 22, with `notguarantee` as the spine).

### 10. Credits, registries and the accounting of nothing
Carbon offsets, from the January 2023 Guardian / Die Zeit / SourceMaterial
analysis of Verra's rainforest programme onward. Why an offset is an accounting
instrument rather than an environmental one, and what the EU's restriction of
offset-based neutrality claims actually changes.

*Draws on:* field notes 3, 9; `record.json` (JBS, KLM, TotalEnergies).

### 11. Who pays the auditor
The economics underneath Part Three. The audited party selects and pays the
auditor; visits are announced; a day or two covers thousands of workers; scope
excludes the subcontractors where the worst work is done. None of this is
corruption. All of it is procurement. The chapter that explains why chapters 8
to 10 keep producing the same result.

*Draws on:* the ECCHR and Clean Clothes documentation; `sources.json`.

## Part Four — What Actually Moves

*Short chapters. No manifesto.*

### 12. When voluntary stopped being enough
The CSDDD, the French Loi de Vigilance and its early court cases, the EU Green
Claims and Empowering Consumers Directives, the 2024 Right to Repair Directive.
Two decades of corporate responsibility as a marketing function, now closing —
slowly, partially, unmistakably. Written without triumphalism, because most of
it is not in force yet.

*Draws on:* field notes 5, 9, 10.

### 13. The vacated rule
The counter-chapter, and the honest one. The FTC's Click-to-Cancel rule was
finalised in 2024, vacated by the Eighth Circuit in July 2025 on procedural
grounds, and reopened for rulemaking in January 2026. Regulation does not
ratchet. A book that ends at chapter 12 would be lying by structure.

*Draws on:* field note 16; `library.json` (FTC negative-option record).

### 14. A method, not a verdict
What a reader can actually do, stated at the size it actually is. The book
refuses to tell anyone whether a specific product is ethical — the same refusal
the site makes — and explains why that refusal is the useful answer: a
conclusion you can reconstruct beats one you have to trust. Ends with the six
checks, which take about a minute each.

*Draws on:* the consumer method in `products/copy.json`; `act.json`.

## Appendix

The reference material as an appendix rather than dispersed through the text: the
36 terms, the 22 certification scopes with their exclusions, the 21 materials,
and the 54 primary authorities. Generated from the same datasets as the site, so
the appendix and the tools cannot drift apart.

This is also the answer to "why buy the book when the site is free": the site is
searchable, the book is arguable. The appendix means the reader does not have to
choose.

## What to be careful about

**The pairing.** Chapter 1 has to earn the animals-and-women structure in its
first few pages or a reviewer will spend the review on it instead of the book.
The defence is structural, not sentimental, and it must be made once, properly,
and then not repeated.

**Scale numbness.** Chapters 5 and 6 are the ones most likely to lose a reader.
Numbers past a certain size stop working. Both need a device that is not a
statistic and not an anecdote about one animal.

**The site's own claims.** Every figure in the book has to survive the same
check the site's do. Fourteen source links in the datasets were dead as of
September 2026 and three claims were wrong. That will happen again over a
manuscript this size, and the answer is a claims file for the book itself —
the same procedure sold in the compliance pack, applied to this.

**Keeping the six shapes doing work.** Chapter 9's taxonomy is the most reusable
thing in the book, and chapters 10 and 11 should lean on it rather than
re-deriving it. Offsets are shape two with a different commodity; ESG scores are
shape four. If those chapters restate the argument instead of applying it, the
taxonomy was decoration.

**Not writing the reassurance apparatus as conspiracy.** Chapter 11 is where
this book either becomes serious or becomes a polemic. Nobody in the audit
industry is a villain; the incentives are ordinary and that is the whole point.
If the reader finishes chapter 11 angry at auditors rather than at the
arrangement, the chapter has failed.

## Order of work

1. **Chapter 8** — written. It is the sample because it is the most
   counterintuitive chapter, it sits at the centre of the argument, and it is
   built on documented cases rather than on interpretation. If it does not work,
   the book does not.
2. **Chapter 9** — written. Together with 8 it establishes the argument of Part
   Three from two directions: the audit that was scoped not to find, and the
   logo that is narrower than it reads.
3. **Chapter 11.** The economics underneath both. With 8 and 9 it completes the
   proposal — the three chapters an agent or a publisher would need to see.
4. **Chapter 1.** Written after Part Three, not before — the thesis is easier to
   state once the strongest evidence for it exists on paper.
5. **Everything else,** in the order the research is already strongest:
   4, 7, 10, 2, 3, 12, 13, 5, 6, 14.

At roughly 5,500 words a chapter, and one chapter a fortnight alongside
everything else, this is a nine to twelve month manuscript. Anyone who tells you
it is a three-month project has not written one.
