# 8. Reading an audit that was never going to find anything

In August 2012, an Italian certification company called RINA Services issued a
certificate to a textile factory in the Baldia Town district of Karachi. The
factory was called Ali Enterprises. It made jeans, mostly for a German discount
retailer. The certificate said the factory complied with SA8000, an
international standard covering child labour, forced labour, health and safety,
freedom of association, working hours and pay.

Three weeks later, on 11 September 2012, a fire started on the ground floor. The
windows had been barred. Most of the exits were locked. More than 250 people
died, many of them in a basement they could not get out of. It remains one of
the deadliest industrial fires in the history of manufacturing anywhere.

There is an obvious way to read this, and it is the wrong one.

The obvious reading is that the audit failed. That somebody was lazy, or
bribed, or incompetent; that a certificate was handed over without the work
being done; that if the auditors had looked properly they would have seen the
barred windows and the locked doors and the single usable stairwell, and 250
people would be alive. On this reading the problem is a bad auditor, and the
solution is better auditors.

I want to argue that this reading is not just incorrect but actively harmful,
because it is the reading that has allowed the social audit industry to survive
thirteen years of this. Every time a certified factory kills its workers, the
industry's answer is that this was an aberration, that lessons have been
learned, that the methodology has been strengthened. And every time, the answer
works, because the alternative explanation has never been put plainly enough.

The alternative explanation is that the audit did not fail. It did what it was
built to do. And the certificate never claimed what its readers believed it
claimed.

---

## What a certificate is for

Start with the commercial question, because everything else follows from it.

A brand in Europe or North America buys from a factory in Pakistan or Bangladesh
or Vietnam. The brand has made public commitments about labour conditions. It
has a code of conduct on its website. It cannot inspect several hundred
factories itself, in several dozen countries, in languages it does not speak,
against standards it has not the expertise to assess. So it buys the assessment
from somebody else.

That somebody else is a certification body or an audit firm. The market is
large, competitive, and priced. And here is the structural fact that everything
in this chapter rests on: **in most of this market, the audited party pays for
the audit.**

Not the brand. Not the workers. Not a regulator. The factory being inspected
selects the inspector, negotiates the fee, and pays it. In the Ali Enterprises
case, the SA8000 audit was carried out not by RINA directly but by a
subcontractor in Karachi, a local firm called RI&CA — a further step of remove
between the company whose name was on the certificate and the person who walked
the floor.

Consider what this arrangement asks of the auditor. They are a supplier. Their
client is the factory. Their client wants a certificate — that is the product
being purchased — and can select a different supplier next year. There is no
regulator to answer to, because SA8000 is a voluntary private standard, not a
law. There is no worker with standing to sue. The only party with an interest in
a rigorous finding is the brand, and the brand is not in the room, is not paying,
and in most cases will never read the full report — only the certificate.

I have written that paragraph without using the word corruption, and I want to
keep it that way for the rest of the chapter. Nothing described above is
corrupt. It is procurement. A supplier delivering what a paying client wants,
within the terms of a contract, is the ordinary functioning of a market. That is
precisely the problem, and it is why replacing individual auditors changes
nothing. You cannot fix an incentive by hiring a better person to sit inside it.

## What the audit actually looked at

The second structural fact is narrower and, once you see it, harder to unsee.

On 24 April 2013, seven months after the Karachi fire, an eight-storey building
outside Dhaka called Rana Plaza collapsed. At least 1,131 people died. The
building had been built without the permits for the floors it had; heavy
generators had been installed on upper floors it was not engineered to carry;
cracks had appeared in the structure the day before, and workers had been sent
back in.

Two of the garment factories inside Rana Plaza — New Wave Style and Phantom
Apparel — had been audited against the BSCI code of conduct. BSCI, then the
Business Social Compliance Initiative, was and remains one of the largest social
auditing programmes in the world. Phantom Apparel's audit had been carried out
by TÜV Rheinland, a German inspection company of substantial reputation.

Neither audit identified that the building was unsafe.

The industry's answer, when this was put to it, was not that the auditors had
been careless. It was more interesting than that, and considerably more
damaging. The answer was that **structural building safety was not within the
scope of a BSCI audit.** The auditors were not looking for it. They were not
qualified to look for it — a social compliance auditor is not a structural
engineer, has no training in load-bearing calculations, and is not asked to
form a view on whether a building will stand up. The scope of the audit was
labour practice: hours, wages, contracts, child labour, freedom of association.
It was, on its own terms, the wrong instrument for the question.

This was, and is, true. It is also a complete description of the problem.

Because nobody buying a garment from a BSCI-member brand had any way of knowing
that. Nobody reading the words "social compliance audit" attached to a factory
understood that the building the factory sat in was outside the frame. The
certificate did not say *we have assessed wages and working hours and have
formed no view whatsoever on whether this structure is safe to stand in*. It
said the factory had been audited. The reader supplied the rest, and the reader
supplied it in the direction the certificate benefited from.

That gap — between what an instrument verifies and what its audience infers —
is not a flaw in social auditing. It is the same gap that runs through every
certification scheme in the next chapter. Leaping Bunny is a real, audited,
rigorous standard, and it does not mean a product contains no animal
ingredients. The Responsible Wool Standard genuinely prohibits mulesing, and it
says nothing about slaughter. GOTS certifies organic fibre and processing, and
does not cover conditions on the farm. In every case the scheme is honest about
its scope in its own documentation, and in every case the scope is narrower than
the logo implies to the person the logo is aimed at.

The schemes are not lying. They are being read.

## The mechanics, briefly

It is worth being concrete about what an audit is, because the word implies a
thoroughness the practice does not have.

A social audit of a large garment factory typically takes one or two days. In
that time the auditor must review documentation — payroll, time records,
contracts, age verification — tour the site, and interview workers. A factory
employing two or three thousand people might have forty or fifty of them
interviewed, on the premises, during working hours.

Visits are usually announced in advance. This is defended on practical grounds:
somebody has to be there with the records. The consequence is that a factory
knows when the auditor is coming.

What follows from that is well documented, in the ECCHR's case files, in the
Clean Clothes Campaign's research, and in the academic literature on supply
chain monitoring. Two sets of books: one for the auditor, one that is real.
Underage workers given the day off. Workers briefed on what to say, sometimes
with a script, sometimes with a threat. Blocked exits unblocked for the visit.
And beneath all of it, the structural evasion that no amount of auditing
addresses: the audited factory subcontracts the work it does not want seen to a
smaller unit down the road that no brand has a contract with, no auditor has a
mandate over, and no certificate covers.

Every one of those is a rational response by a factory to an inspection it is
paying for and needs to pass.

## Why brands keep buying it

There is a question the previous section leaves open, and the whole chapter
depends on answering it. If the audit is commissioned by the wrong party, scoped
to the wrong questions, announced in advance, and defeated by a subcontract, why
do brands keep paying for it? They are not stupid. Many of the people running
these programmes are sincere. Some of them have been arguing internally for
years that the system does not work.

The uncomfortable answer is that the audit is not primarily a mechanism for
finding out what is happening in a factory. It is a mechanism for establishing
that the brand did not know.

Consider the position of a brand's general counsel. The risk is not that a
factory in the supply chain is unsafe — that is close to certain across a
network of several hundred sites, and everyone involved understands it. The risk
is that the brand is found to have been aware of it. Awareness is what converts
a supplier's failure into the brand's liability, in litigation, in a regulatory
proceeding, and in the press.

An audit programme is an extraordinarily efficient answer to that risk. It
produces a documented, dated, third-party record of due diligence. It
demonstrates that the brand did not ignore the question — it commissioned a
professional assessment, from a reputable firm, against an international
standard, and the assessment came back clean. If something then goes wrong, the
brand is not a company that looked away. It is a company that was misled by its
supplier, along with everybody else.

This is why the scope limitation in the previous section is not an oversight
that the industry has failed to correct in thirteen years. A narrow scope is
easier to pass, cheaper to assess, and — this is the part that matters —
produces a clean document more reliably. A programme that regularly found
serious problems would be a programme that regularly generated written evidence
of brand awareness. The incentive does not run towards rigour. It runs towards
a defensible paper trail, which is a different product with a superficially
similar shape.

I am not claiming this is anybody's stated intention. I have never seen a
document in which a company sets out to buy ignorance, and I do not think such
a document exists. What I am describing is what the arrangement selects for. Over
a decade, across thousands of factories and dozens of schemes, a market in which
the clean report is the valuable output will produce clean reports. Nobody has to
decide that. It is what the money does.

Which is also why the industry's standard response to a disaster — better
methodology, unannounced visits, worker hotlines, more frequent audits — has
been sincerely attempted and has changed the outcome so little. Each of those
reforms improves the instrument. None of them alters who pays for it or what
the document is for. A more accurate audit that still functions as a liability
shield is a more accurate liability shield.

The test of any proposed fix is therefore simple, and it is worth applying to
whatever your favourite brand announced this year: **does it change what happens
when something is found?** If a finding produces a remediation obligation the
brand cannot decline, enforceable by somebody other than the brand, then the
programme has teeth. If a finding produces a corrective action plan that the
brand monitors itself, on a timetable it sets, with no consequence for
non-completion, then the finding is documentation. That is the entire
distinction, and it is why the Accord worked and BSCI did not.

## Thirteen years

Ali Enterprises has produced almost no consequence for the people who certified
it.

In 2018, a coalition of eight human rights, labour and consumer organisations
filed a complaint against RINA with the Italian government's OECD National
Contact Point in Rome. RINA has publicly defended its conduct. A separate case
against KiK, the German retailer that was the factory's main customer, was
brought in the German courts by four families, and was dismissed in 2019 on
limitation grounds under Pakistani law. Compensation for the families came
eventually, through a settlement negotiated with the ILO and pressure from
labour organisations, rather than through any finding against anybody.

The SA8000 standard still exists. RINA still certifies. BSCI, now amfori,
still operates one of the largest social auditing programmes in the world. The
number of certified factories has grown considerably since 2013.

What changed after Rana Plaza was not the audit industry. It was one thing
happening alongside it. The Accord on Fire and Building Safety in Bangladesh, a
binding agreement between brands and trade unions, did something social audits
structurally cannot: it made brands legally accountable for remediation, gave
unions enforceable standing, published its inspection findings, and — critically
— hired structural engineers to assess buildings, because that is what the
question required. It was not a better audit. It was a different instrument,
with the paying party, the enforcing party and the assessed party rearranged.

That is the shape of the lesson, and it is not a comfortable one for anyone who
wants a label to solve this. The problem with social auditing was never the
quality of the auditing. It was who commissioned it, what it was permitted to
examine, and what happened when it found something.

## What to do with a certificate

None of this means a certificate is worthless. That conclusion is as lazy as the
one it replaces, and it leads somewhere unhelpful — a reader who trusts nothing
makes exactly the same purchasing decisions as a reader who trusts everything.

It means a certificate is evidence of a specific, bounded thing, and the only
useful question is which thing. So:

**Find the published scope.** Every serious scheme publishes what it verifies.
It is usually a PDF, it is usually dull, and it is usually narrower than you
expected. The scope is the claim; the logo is the marketing.

**Ask what it excludes.** This is the question the scheme's own documentation
will answer and its marketing will not. A scheme that is straightforward about
its exclusions is a better scheme than one that is vague about them.

**Ask who paid.** An assessment commissioned and paid for by the party being
assessed is a different kind of document from one commissioned by a regulator, a
union, or a brand with liability attached. Not a worthless one. A different one.

**Ask what happened when something was found.** A scheme with no published
record of decertification is a scheme that has never said no.

None of those four questions require expertise. They require about ten minutes
and a willingness to read the boring document. The reason they are not asked
more often is not that they are hard. It is that the certificate is designed to
make asking them feel unnecessary, and it is very good at that — which is, in
the end, what it was built for.
