# 9. What the logo does not cover

There is a rabbit on a great many bottles.

The Leaping Bunny is one of the more rigorous marks in this book. It is run by
Cruelty Free International, it requires a fixed cut-off date after which no
animal testing may have occurred, it covers the finished product *and* every
ingredient, it extends to the brand's suppliers and to any third party acting
for them, and it is independently audited with ongoing supplier monitoring.
Almost nothing else on a cosmetics shelf is verified that thoroughly.

It does not mean the product contains no animals.

A Leaping Bunny product can contain beeswax, lanolin, carmine — carmine being a
red pigment made from crushed cochineal insects, roughly seventy thousand of
them per pound. All of that is entirely compatible with the certification,
because the certification is about testing, and testing is not ingredients.

Cruelty Free International says so. It is not hidden, it is not a technicality
they would rather you missed, and they have never claimed otherwise. The
sentence "does not mean vegan" appears in their own material.

And yet a very large number of people buy that bottle believing the rabbit
covers both, because the rabbit is a *rabbit*, and a rabbit on a face cream
means, in the ordinary reading of the world, that no animal was harmed for
this. The logo is doing work its standard never claimed to do, and the work is
being done in the reader's head rather than on the label.

This chapter is about that gap, across twenty-two schemes. And the finding is
not that the schemes are dishonest. Most are careful, several are excellent,
and every one of them publishes its own scope. The finding is that the gaps
have **shapes** — a small number of recurring forms — and that once you can
name the shape, you can predict where any certification you have never seen
before will turn out to be narrower than it looks.

---

## Shape one: the adjacent virtue

The commonest gap, and the one the rabbit is an example of. A scheme verifies
one virtue. The buyer hears that virtue *and its neighbour*.

- **Leaping Bunny** certifies no testing. It does not certify no animal
  ingredients.
- **Certified Vegan**, the sunflower run by the Vegan Society and Vegan Action,
  does the reverse: no animal-derived ingredients, reviewed on application. But
  vegan does not automatically mean cruelty-free unless the certifier also
  covers testing, and not every vegan mark does.
- **OEKO-TEX Standard 100** is perhaps the starkest case. It tests a finished
  textile against a list of harmful substances and confirms it falls below
  defined limits. That is a chemical safety label for the article you are
  holding. It is not an organic standard, not an animal-welfare standard, not a
  labour standard, and not a measure of environmental footprint. It says
  nothing whatever about who made the garment or how. On a shelf of tags it
  looks like the reassuring one.
- **bluesign** manages chemical inputs across textile manufacturing —
  restricting hazardous substances, improving resource use at factory level. It
  does not address animal welfare and does not address garment-worker wages. It
  is an environmental management system, and it is a good one, and it is not an
  ethics-of-labour mark.

Notice what these four have in common. Each is *rigorous* within its subject.
The gap is not slackness. The gap is that virtue in the buyer's mind is a
single undifferentiated quality — *good* — and every certification scheme in
existence has to pick one thing and verify that thing, because verifying
everything is not a scheme, it is an aspiration.

A label can be exact and still mislead, if what it is exact about is not what
the reader was asking.

---

## Shape two: the mixing

This is the one that genuinely shocks people, and I want to be precise about it
because the precision is the point.

Several commodity certifications operate supply-chain models that permit
certified and uncertified material to be blended. The strongest models keep
them apart. **Segregated** means the certified material is kept physically
separate all the way through. **Identity Preserved** goes further and traces it
back to a single certified source.

Then there are the weaker models.

**Mass Balance** allows certified and non-certified material to be physically
mixed, with the volumes tracked administratively: a manufacturer that bought
ten tonnes of certified palm oil may sell ten tonnes of product labelled as
certified, even though what is in any particular container is a blend.

**Book & Claim**, sometimes called credits, severs the link entirely. A buyer
purchases certificates from a certified producer somewhere in the world, and
those certificates are matched against the volume they sell. The certified
material and the sold material need never have been in the same country.

The **Roundtable on Sustainable Palm Oil** offers all of these. So does
**Rainforest Alliance**, which uses mass balance for cocoa. **FSC** has an
equivalent: FSC Mix blends certified, recycled and "controlled" material, and
only **FSC 100%** is what the shopper assumes the logo means.

None of this is fraud. Mass balance exists because commodity supply chains
physically cannot segregate every stream, and because a credit system creates
demand for certified production where segregation is impossible. There is a
real argument that it does more good than a purist alternative that nobody
adopts. I am not competent to settle that argument and this chapter does not
try to.

What I am certain of is this: **the model is a material fact about what you are
buying, and it is almost never on the front of the pack.** A shopper choosing
the certified chocolate over the uncertified one is making a decision, and the
decision is meaningfully different depending on whether the cocoa in that bar
was certified or merely accounted for. Both are defensible schemes. They are
not the same purchase.

And both carry the same logo.

---

## Shape three: the threshold

A logo is a binary. The standard behind it is usually a scale.

- **Global Recycled Standard** requires a minimum of **20%** recycled content
  for a product to carry the logo. Fifty per cent or more is needed for the
  fuller claims. A garment that is four-fifths virgin material can display the
  mark.
- **GOTS** has two grades that most people do not know are two grades. "Organic"
  requires 95% or more certified organic fibre. "Made with organic" covers 70
  to 94%. These are visually similar labels describing materially different
  products.
- **Cradle to Cradle Certified** runs from Bronze to Platinum across material
  health, reuse, renewable energy, water and social fairness. A Bronze is a much
  weaker claim than a Gold, and the logo alone does not tell you which you are
  looking at.
- **Fairtrade** certifies the ingredient, not necessarily the whole product. On
  a composite product — a biscuit, a chocolate bar with fillings — a percentage
  applies, and that percentage is not the biscuit.

The threshold shape is the most quietly effective of the six, because a
threshold is invisible. You cannot look at a logo and see the floor underneath
it. Twenty per cent recycled and ninety per cent recycled are, to the eye at
point of sale, the same claim.

---

## Shape four: the wrong subject

Sometimes the certification is real, rigorous and current, and it is simply not
about the thing in your hand.

**B Corp** certifies a *company*. It is a verified assessment of social and
environmental performance, accountability and transparency, with a minimum
score of 80 and re-certification every three years. It is a serious process.
And it rates the organisation as a whole — not any single product, and not
specifically animal welfare or veganism. A B Corp can, entirely consistently,
sell a product you would not buy.

**Fair Trade Certified**, the US scheme run by Fair Trade USA — which is a
different organisation from Fairtrade International, with different standards
and different governance, a distinction almost nobody makes at a shelf —
certifies specific products or facilities. Not the company. The rest of the
brand may be entirely unaudited.

**Fair Wear Foundation** is the most interesting case of the four, and the most
honest. It assesses whether a clothing brand is a *member working to improve*
labour conditions, through a Brand Performance Check. It rates effort and
management practice. It is explicitly not a pass/fail guarantee that every
factory is fine. Fair Wear says this plainly. It is a genuinely useful signal —
a brand submitting to that scrutiny is behaving differently from one that is
not — and it is a signal about the brand's conduct, not about your jacket.

**ZQ Merino** and **Regenerative Organic Certified** are both farm-level. ZQ
covers animal welfare including non-mulesing, environmental management and
social responsibility on accredited farms — for the *fibre*. It does not cover
the dyeing, the sewing, or the labour conditions wherever the garment was
actually assembled. ROC builds on organic certification with soil health,
animal welfare and farmworker fairness, and is one of the stricter farm-level
marks in existence. It still certifies how a raw material was grown, not how
the final product was manufactured or by whom.

The reader's error here is a category error, and it is an extremely reasonable
one: a certification appearing on a product is naturally read as a statement
about the product.

---

## Shape five: the evidence behind it

Two logos can make an apparently identical claim on the same shelf and rest on
completely different kinds of proof.

**PETA's Beauty Without Bunnies** programme has two marks: "Animal Test-Free"
and "Animal Test-Free & Vegan". The base logo rests on a **signed statement of
assurance** from the brand that it and its suppliers do not test on animals. It
is a pledge.

**Leaping Bunny** requires a fixed cut-off date, supplier monitoring, and
independent audits.

Both are widely used. Both are honestly described by their operators. Both
appear on cosmetics packaging in the same size and in the same place. One is a
company's own word, systematically collected. The other is an audited
verification. That is not a small difference, and there is nothing on the front
of the pack that expresses it.

I want to be careful here, because this is the point at which a chapter like
this tips into cynicism. A pledge is not worthless. A public, written
commitment creates a documented statement a company can be held to, and
companies do change behaviour to be able to sign one. But a pledge and an audit
are different instruments answering different questions, and only one of them
survives the question *how do you know?*

## Shape six: the gap that cannot be closed

The last shape is not a limitation of the scheme. It is a limitation of what
certification can do at all, and the schemes in question are, without
exception, straightforward about it.

**RSPCA Assured** verifies farm-animal welfare against species-specific
standards covering the animal's life, its transport, and its slaughter, with
assessment visits. **Certified Humane** verifies welfare standards including
space, no cages for hens, and no routine antibiotics, with annual third-party
inspection. **RDS and RWS**, the Responsible Down and Responsible Wool
Standards, trace down and wool through the supply chain against welfare
criteria: RDS prohibits live-plucking and force-feeding; RWS addresses sheep
welfare and land management, and prohibits mulesing.

Every one of those is a real improvement on the unlabelled alternative, and I
would rather a hen was in a Certified Humane system than not. But none of them
is a statement that no animal was farmed, and none is a statement that no
animal was killed. RSPCA Assured is a welfare scheme; it is not organic, not
free-range by default, and it does not mean the animal was not slaughtered.
Certified Humane is a welfare label, not a no-slaughter label.

They are not pretending otherwise. The word is *welfare*, and welfare has
always meant the conditions of a life, not the absence of an ending.

The gap here is between what the scheme can offer and what a buyer standing in
front of a chilled cabinet, who would rather not think about this at all, would
like the label to mean. No standard can close that. Only a decision can, and it
is not the label's job to make it.

---

## Why the gaps are there

It would be convenient to conclude that certification is a racket. It is not,
and the evidence in this chapter does not support it.

Certification works by being narrow. A standard is auditable precisely because
it has a boundary — a defined set of criteria, a defined stage of the supply
chain, a defined test. Widen the scope far enough and you arrive at a claim
nobody can verify and therefore nobody can be held to, which is where
"sustainable" and "eco-friendly" already are and why chapter 2 exists. The
narrowness is not a defect in certification. It is the mechanism.

The problem is what happens at the point of translation. A standard is a
document of many pages, written for a technical audience, published openly and
read by roughly nobody. A logo is a rabbit, eleven millimetres across, on a
bottle held by somebody deciding between two moisturisers in about four
seconds.

Everything the standard is careful about has to survive that compression, and
almost none of it does. What survives is the shape of an animal and a general
impression of decency. The scheme has not lied. The compression has.

And here is the part that ought to make everyone uncomfortable, including the
schemes: **the compression is commercially useful to the licensee.** A brand
paying to display a mark is buying the impression, not the document. If the
impression were accurate to the standard, it would be a narrower and less
appealing impression, and the mark would be worth less. Nobody has to intend
this for it to be true — it is the same shape as the incentive in chapter 8,
and it produces the same result: a system in which the accurate version is the
one that nobody chooses to make prominent.

---

## Four questions

The practical upshot is short, and it is the same list as the previous chapter
because the questions do not change with the instrument.

**What is the subject?** Company, brand, facility, product, or raw material?
This single question separates B Corp from Fairtrade from ROC, and it is the
one that produces the most misreadings.

**What is the supply-chain model?** Segregated, identity preserved, mass
balance, or credits? If the pack does not say, the answer is more often the
weaker one, because the stronger ones are worth advertising.

**What is the threshold?** Twenty per cent or ninety? Bronze or Platinum?
Organic or made-with-organic? A logo is a binary and the standard behind it is
almost never one.

**How is it verified?** A signed pledge, a desk review, an announced audit, or
an unannounced one? Chapter 8 is what happens when nobody asks.

Every one of those is answered in the scheme's own published documentation,
free, by the organisation that runs it. This is worth sitting with. **The
information is not being withheld.** It is public, it is written down, and the
schemes will hand it to you.

It just isn't on the bottle. And what is on the bottle is a rabbit.
